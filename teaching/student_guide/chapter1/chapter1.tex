\section{The experiment: a CRISPR screen}
%=====================================================================

Each cell in your body has about 20{,}000 genes. A gene is a set of
instructions. Scientists want to know which genes a cell needs, for example
to survive a cancer drug.

A \textbf{CRISPR screen} tests thousands of genes at the same time. It works
in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Scientists make a \textbf{library} of short pieces of RNA, called
  \textbf{guides}. Each guide switches off one gene.
\item They put one guide into each cell. Now each cell has one gene switched
  off.
\item They grow the cells. Some cells grow under normal conditions
  (\textbf{control}). Other cells grow with a drug (\textbf{drug}).
\item A sequencing machine reads the guides in each group. The machine
  counts how many times it reads each guide. This number is a
  \textbf{read count}.
\end{enumerate}

If cells need a gene to survive the drug, the cells that lose that gene die.
Their guide becomes rare in the drug group. So the guide count goes down.

\begin{remember}
BARCS does not look at cells directly. It looks at read counts. A guide that
becomes rare tells us that its gene is important.
\end{remember}

Scientists repeat each group more than one time. Each repeat is a
\textbf{replicate}. Each sample that the machine reads is a \textbf{library}.
In this guide, we use four libraries: two control replicates and two drug
replicates.

%=====================================================================
\section{Step 1: Turn counts into proportions}
%=====================================================================

Library~2 has more reads than library~1 because the machine read it more.
So we cannot compare raw counts. We divide each count by its library total.
The result is a \textbf{proportion}:
\[
p = \frac{K}{N}.
\]

Proportions are very small numbers. So we multiply by one million. The
result is the count \textbf{per million} reads.

\begin{worked}[ 1: proportions]
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
Library 1: & $p = 180 \div 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.000180$ & $= 180$ per million\\
Library 2: & $p = 240 \div 1{,}200{,}000 = 0.000200$ & $= 200$ per million\\
Library 3: & $p = 120 \div 1{,}000{,}000 = 0.000120$ & $= 120$ per million\\
Library 4: & $p = \phantom{0}80 \div \phantom{0,}800{,}000 = 0.000100$ & $= 100$ per million\\
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
The two control libraries give 180 and 200 per million. The two drug
libraries give 120 and 100 per million. The guide is less common with the
drug.
\end{worked}

\begin{symbols}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{0.78\linewidth}@{}}
$p$ & The proportion of all reads that belong to the guide. It is always
      between 0 and 1.\\
\end{tabular}
\end{symbols}

%=====================================================================
\section{Step 2: Compare the groups with a fold change}
%=====================================================================

We want one number that tells how much the guide changes. First, we combine
the replicates of each group. We add their counts and add their totals:

\[
p_{\text{control}} = \frac{180+240}{1{,}000{,}000+1{,}200{,}000}
 = \frac{420}{2{,}200{,}000} = 0.000191 \;(191 \text{ per million}),
\]
\[
p_{\text{drug}} = \frac{120+80}{1{,}000{,}000+800{,}000}
 = \frac{200}{1{,}800{,}000} = 0.000111 \;(111 \text{ per million}).
\]

The \textbf{fold change} is the drug proportion divided by the control
proportion:
\[
\text{fold change} = \frac{p_{\text{drug}}}{p_{\text{control}}}
 = \frac{0.000111}{0.000191} = 0.58.
\]

A fold change of 0.58 means that the guide keeps only 58\% of its control
level. It loses 42\%.

\begin{remember}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item Fold change $= 1$: no change.
\item Fold change $< 1$: the guide becomes rarer. Cells may need this gene.
\item Fold change $> 1$: the guide becomes more common. Losing this gene
  may help the cells.
\end{itemize}
\end{remember}

\subsection{Logarithms make changes symmetric}

A fold change of 2 means ``double''. A fold change of 0.5 means ``half''.
These changes have the same size, but the numbers 2 and 0.5 do not look
equal. A \textbf{logarithm} fixes this problem.

The \textbf{base-2 logarithm}, $\log_2$, tells how many times you double.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Fold change & 0.25 & 0.5 & 1 & 2 & 4\\
$\log_2$(fold change) & $-2$ & $-1$ & 0 & 1 & 2\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Now ``double'' is $+1$ and ``half'' is $-1$. The sizes look equal.

\begin{worked}[ 2: log fold change]
Our fold change is 0.58. It is between 0.5 and 1. So $\log_2(0.58)$ is
between $-1$ and 0. A calculator gives $\log_2(0.58) = -0.78$.

The guide drops by 0.78 ``halvings''.
\end{worked}

BARCS uses the \textbf{natural logarithm}, $\ln$. It counts powers of the
number $e = 2.718\ldots$ instead of powers of 2. The idea is the same. To
change back from $\ln$, you raise $e$ to that power: if $\ln(x) = b$, then
$x = e^{b}$. For our guide, $\ln(0.58) = -0.54$.

%=====================================================================
\section{Step 3: Odds and log-odds}
%=====================================================================

BARCS works with \textbf{odds} instead of proportions. The odds compare the
reads that belong to the guide with the reads that do not:
\[
\text{odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}.
\]
The \textbf{log-odds} is the natural logarithm of the odds:
\[
\text{log-odds} = \ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).
\]

\begin{worked}[ 3: odds and log-odds]
Control: $p = 0.000191$. So
$\text{odds} = 0.000191 \div 0.999809 = 0.000191$.

The odds and the proportion are almost equal. This is true for all small
proportions, because $1-p$ is almost 1.

Log-odds for control: $\ln(0.000191) = -8.56$.\\
Log-odds for drug: $\ln(0.000111) = -9.10$.\\
Difference: $-9.10 - (-8.56) = -0.54$.

The difference of the log-odds is $-0.54$. This is the same as
$\ln(\text{fold change})$ from Step~2.
\end{worked}

\begin{remember}
For guides, a difference in log-odds is almost exactly the natural log of
the fold change. So you can read BARCS results as log fold changes.
\end{remember}

Why use log-odds at all? A proportion must stay between 0 and 1. A log-odds
can be any number, positive or negative. This property lets BARCS add
effects together safely, as Step~5 shows.

%=====================================================================
\section{Step 4: Two kinds of noise}
%=====================================================================

The two control libraries gave 180 and 200 per million. They are not equal.
Replicates always differ a little. We call this difference
\textbf{noise}. BARCS must know how big the noise is. Then it can tell a
real change from a chance change.

Guide counts have two kinds of noise:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Counting noise.} The machine reads a random sample of all
  the guides. Think of a bag with a million marbles. You take out a handful.
  You do not get exactly the same number of red marbles each time.
\item \textbf{Biological noise.} Each replicate is a different group of
  living cells. The cells grow a little differently each time.
\end{enumerate}

BARCS measures the size of the noise with the \textbf{variance}. The
variance is the average squared distance of a value from its expected
value. The \textbf{standard deviation} is the square root of the variance.
It is the typical distance in the same units as the counts.

This step builds the variance formula of BARCS in four parts. In all
formulas, the sign $\times$ means ``multiply''. So $N \times \mu$ means
``$N$ multiplied by $\mu$''.

\begin{symbols}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{0.78\linewidth}@{}}
$N$ & The library total, as before. Our example uses
      $N = 1{,}000{,}000$.\\
$\mu$ & ``mu''. The proportion of the guide that we expect in the library.
        For the control group, our best value is $\mu = 0.000191$. It is
        like $p$ from Step~1, but it is the expected value, not the value
        that we measure.\\
$\rho$ & ``rho''. A number that measures the biological noise. If
         $\rho = 0$, only counting noise exists. A bigger $\rho$ means more
         biological noise. Part~3 explains it.\\
\end{tabular}
\end{symbols}

\subsection{Part 1: The expected count}

The machine reads $N$ reads. Each read belongs to our guide with the chance
$\mu$. So we expect
\[
\text{expected count} = N \times \mu .
\]

\begin{worked}[ 4a: expected count]
$N \times \mu = 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.000191 = 191$ reads.

The real counts are near 191, but not exactly 191. The variance tells how
far they normally are from 191.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Part 2: Counting noise}

Start with \textbf{one read}. It belongs to our guide (value 1) or it does
not (value 0). We need the variance of this one value. We do it in three
steps: first with five marbles, then the reason for the squares, then the
general formula.

\paragraph{Step A: Five marbles.} Put five marbles in a bag: one red and
four blue. Give the red marble the value 1 and each blue marble the value 0.
So the chance of red is $\mu = 1/5 = 0.2$. The average value is
$(1 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0) / 5 = 0.2$. This average is $\mu$.

Now find the distance of each marble from the average:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Marble & Value & Distance (value $-$ 0.2) & Squared distance\\
\midrule
red & 1 & $+0.8$ & 0.64\\
blue & 0 & $-0.2$ & 0.04\\
blue & 0 & $-0.2$ & 0.04\\
blue & 0 & $-0.2$ & 0.04\\
blue & 0 & $-0.2$ & 0.04\\
\midrule
Sum & & $0.8 - 0.8 = 0$ & 0.80\\
Average (sum $\div$ 5) & & 0 & \textbf{0.16}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Look at the distance column. The positive and the negative distances cancel.
Their sum is 0. This is always true: the distances from an average always
add up to 0. So the plain average distance cannot measure the spread. It is
0 for every bag.

To stop the cancelling, we square each distance. A square is never negative.
The average squared distance is $0.80 / 5 = 0.16$. This is the
\textbf{variance} of one marble.

\paragraph{Step B: Why squares, and not only the size of the distance?}
We could also drop the minus signs and use the size of each distance. But
BARCS needs a measure that \textbf{adds up}: the spread of a sum of
independent values must be the sum of their spreads. Part~2 adds up a
million reads, and Part~4 adds two kinds of noise. Squares have this
property. Sizes do not.

\begin{worked}[ 4b: two coins]
Throw two fair coins. Heads $= 1$, tails $= 0$. Each coin has $\mu = 0.5$.
The four results are equally likely.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Coins & Total & Distance from 1 & Squared distance\\
\midrule
tails, tails & 0 & $-1$ & 1\\
tails, heads & 1 & 0 & 0\\
heads, tails & 1 & 0 & 0\\
heads, heads & 2 & $+1$ & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Variance of the total:} $(1 + 0 + 0 + 1) / 4 = 0.5$.
\item \textbf{Variance of one coin:} distances are $\pm 0.5$, squared
  0.25. Two coins: $0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$. \textbf{The same.} Squares add up.
\item \textbf{Size of distance, total:} $(1 + 0 + 0 + 1) / 4 = 0.5$.
\item \textbf{Size of distance, one coin:} 0.5. Two coins: $0.5 + 0.5 = 1$.
  \textbf{Not the same} as 0.5. Sizes do not add up.
\end{itemize}
\end{worked}

The statistician Ronald Fisher gave the name \emph{variance} to the average
squared distance in 1918. He chose it because the variances of independent
values add up.\footnote{R.\,A. Fisher (1918). The correlation between relatives on the supposition of Mendelian inheritance. \emph{Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh} 52: 399--433.} The idea of squared distances is older. In
1805 Adrien-Marie Legendre fitted lines by making the sum of squared
distances as small as possible (``least squares''), and Carl Friedrich Gauss
used the same idea.\footnote{S.\,M. Stigler (1986). \emph{The History of Statistics}. Harvard University Press, chapter~1.} BARCS still uses least squares in
Chapter~2.

\paragraph{Step C: The general formula.} Now replace ``one marble in five''
by any chance $\mu$. The table has only two kinds of rows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Value & How often (chance) & Distance from $\mu$ & Squared distance\\
\midrule
1 & $\mu$ & $1-\mu$ & $(1-\mu)^2$\\
0 & $1-\mu$ & $-\mu$ & $\mu^2$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The average squared distance is each squared distance multiplied by how
often it happens, then added:
\[
\text{variance of one read} = \mu \times (1-\mu)^2 + (1-\mu) \times \mu^2 .
\]
In the five-marble bag: $0.2 \times 0.8^2 + 0.8 \times 0.2^2 = 0.128 +
0.032 = 0.16$. This is the same 0.16 as in the table. (The table gave
$0.64 \times \frac{1}{5} + 0.04 \times \frac{4}{5}$.)

\paragraph{Step D: From the long formula to $\mu \times (1-\mu)$.}
We now open every bracket, one step at a time. The right column does the
same step with $\mu = 0.2$, so you can check each line.

\textbf{First, open the square.} A square means ``multiply by itself'':
$(1-\mu)^2 = (1-\mu) \times (1-\mu)$. Multiply each part of the first
bracket by each part of the second bracket:
\[
(1-\mu) \times (1-\mu) = 1 \times 1 \;-\; 1 \times \mu \;-\; \mu \times 1
 \;+\; \mu \times \mu = 1 - 2 \times \mu + \mu^2 .
\]
Check: $0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.64$, and $1 - 2 \times 0.2 + 0.04 = 0.64$.

\needspace{16\baselineskip}
\textbf{Then, expand everything.}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r@{\;}l l@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{With letters} & \textbf{With $\mu = 0.2$}\\
\midrule
1. & $\mu \times (1-\mu)^2 + (1-\mu) \times \mu^2$
   & $0.2 \times 0.64 + 0.8 \times 0.04$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad The formula from Step C.}\\
2. & $\mu \times (1 - 2 \times \mu + \mu^2) + (1-\mu) \times \mu^2$
   & $0.2 \times 0.64 + 0.8 \times 0.04$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad Put in the opened square.}\\
3. & $(\mu - 2 \times \mu^2 + \mu^3) + (\mu^2 - \mu^3)$
   & $(0.2 - 0.08 + 0.008) + (0.04 - 0.008)$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad Multiply $\mu$ into the first bracket and $\mu^2$ into the second.}\\
4. & $\mu - 2 \times \mu^2 + \mu^2 + \mu^3 - \mu^3$
   & $0.2 - 0.08 + 0.04 + 0.008 - 0.008$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad Put the same powers next to each other.}\\
5. & $\mu - \mu^2$
   & $0.2 - 0.04 = 0.16$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad $-2 \times \mu^2 + \mu^2 = -\mu^2$, and $+\mu^3 - \mu^3 = 0$.}\\
6. & $\mu \times (1-\mu)$
   & $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$\\
   & \multicolumn{2}{@{}l@{}}{\footnotesize\quad Take $\mu$ out: $\mu - \mu \times \mu = \mu \times (1 - \mu)$.}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every line gives 0.16. So the long formula and the short formula are always
equal:
\[
\mu \times (1-\mu)^2 + (1-\mu) \times \mu^2 = \mu \times (1-\mu).
\]

\textbf{A shorter way.} Both parts of the long formula contain
$\mu \times (1-\mu)$. Take this common part out:
\[
\mu \times (1-\mu) \times \bigl[(1-\mu) + \mu\bigr] = \mu \times (1-\mu)
\times 1 = \mu \times (1-\mu).
\]
The bracket is 1 because $(1-\mu) + \mu = 1$. Check: $0.2 \times 0.8 \times
(0.8 + 0.2) = 0.16$.

\begin{worked}[ 4c: 100 reads]
\textbf{100 reads:} The reads are independent. So their variances add up
(Step~B). 100 reads with $\mu = 0.2$ have the variance $100 \times 0.16 =
16$. The expected count is $100 \times 0.2 = 20$. The standard deviation is
$\sqrt{16} = 4$.

\textbf{Check by repeating.} We took 100 marbles ten times. The red counts
were 17, 19, 21, 25, 17, 25, 27, 22, 21 and 14. They are near 20, and they
usually differ from 20 by about 4.
\end{worked}

For $N$ reads, the variances of the $N$ reads add up. So the counting noise
is
\[
\text{counting noise} = N \times \mu \times (1-\mu).
\]
For our guide: $1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.000191 \times 0.999809 = 191$.

\subsection{Part 3: Biological noise --- the proportion wobbles}

Counting noise assumes that every replicate has exactly the same true
proportion $\mu$. But each replicate is a different group of living cells.
Some cells grow a little faster, and some die by chance. So the true
proportion of the guide is a little different in each replicate.

\paragraph{What is the wobble?} We write $p$ for the true proportion of the
guide in one replicate. (This is the proportion in the cells, before the
machine reads anything.) Over many replicates, the average of $p$ is $\mu$.
The \textbf{wobble} is how far $p$ moves away from $\mu$ from one replicate
to the next. We measure it with the same rule as every variance: the average
squared distance. We call it $V$:
\[
V = \text{average of } (p - \mu)^2 .
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:wobble} shows four replicates. The true proportion is 0.1 in
two of them and 0.3 in the other two. The average is $\mu = 0.2$. Each
distance is 0.1, each squared distance is 0.01, so the wobble is $V = 0.01$.
If every replicate had $p = 0.2$, all the arrows would have length 0, and
the wobble would be $V = 0$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\input{\guideroot common/figs/fig_wobble.tex}
\caption{The wobble. Each dot is the true proportion $p$ in one replicate.
Each arrow is its distance from the average $\mu$. The wobble $V$ is the
average of the squared distances.}
\label{fig:wobble}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Where $\rho$ comes from: two reads from the same replicate.}
The machine reads many reads from one replicate. Take two of them. Ask this
question: \emph{if the first read is our guide, is the second read more
likely to be our guide too?}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Without a wobble: no.} Every replicate has $p = \mu$. Each read
  is our guide with the chance $\mu$, whatever the other read is. The chance
  that both reads are our guide is $\mu \times \mu$.
\item \textbf{With a wobble: yes.} If the first read is our guide, this
  replicate probably has a high $p$. So the second read is also more likely
  to be our guide. Reads from the same replicate \textbf{move together}.
\end{itemize}

Scientists measure how much two values move together with the
\textbf{correlation}. A correlation of 0 means that the two values are
unrelated. A correlation of 1 means that they are always the same. The
number $\rho$ is exactly this: \textbf{the correlation between two reads
from the same replicate}. That is also why it has the name $\rho$: it is
the usual letter for a correlation.\footnote{In statistics, $\rho$ is
called the \emph{intra-class correlation}: the correlation of two trials in
the same group. The beta-binomial model, in which the true proportion
wobbles between groups, was described by J.\,G. Skellam (1948), \emph{Journal
of the Royal Statistical Society B} 10: 257--261.}

Now we calculate it. We do it in three steps.

\textbf{Step 1: the chance that both reads are our guide.} First look
inside \emph{one} replicate, with the true proportion $p$. Each read is our
guide with the chance $p$. When $p$ is fixed, the two reads do not affect
each other, like two throws of a coin. So the chance that both are our
guide is $p \times p$. For example, with $p = 0.3$ it is
$0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$.

Now look at \emph{all} replicates. Each replicate has its own $p$. So the
chance that both reads are our guide is the average of $p \times p$ over the
replicates. In case B, half of the replicates give $0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.01$
and half give $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$. The average is
$(0.01 + 0.09)/2 = 0.05$. Without a wobble, it would be
$0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.04$. Table~\ref{tab:both} shows the three cases of
Figure~\ref{fig:rho}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
Case ($\mu = 0.2$) & Both reads are & If there were & Extra & Correlation\\
& our guide & no wobble ($\mu \times \mu$) & (= covariance) & $\rho$ = extra $\div$ 0.16\\
\midrule
A. $p = 0.2$ always & $0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.04$ & 0.04 & 0 & 0\\
B. $p = 0.1$ or $0.3$ & $(0.01 + 0.09)/2 = 0.05$ & 0.04 & 0.01 & 0.0625\\
C. $p = 0$ or $1$ & $0.8 \times 0 + 0.2 \times 1 = 0.2$ & 0.04 & 0.16 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Two reads from the same replicate. The wobble makes ``both reads
are our guide'' more likely than $\mu \times \mu$. The extra is the

\label{tab:both}
\end{table}

\textbf{Step 2: the extra is the covariance, and it equals $V$.} The extra
over $\mu \times \mu$ shows how much the two reads move together. Its name
is the \textbf{covariance}. It is the average of (distance of read 1 from
$\mu$) $\times$ (distance of read 2 from $\mu$). Call the two reads $X_1$
and $X_2$ (each is 1 or 0). Open the brackets:
\begin{align*}
\text{covariance} &= \text{average of } (X_1 - \mu) \times (X_2 - \mu)\\
&= \text{average of } \bigl(X_1 \times X_2 - \mu \times X_2 - X_1 \times \mu + \mu \times \mu\bigr)\\
&= \text{average of } X_1 \times X_2 - \mu \times \mu - \mu \times \mu + \mu \times \mu\\
&= \text{(chance that both are our guide)} - \mu \times \mu .
\end{align*}
The third line uses that the average of each read is $\mu$. The last line
needs one more fact. The product $X_1 \times X_2$ has only four possible
results:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cccl@{}}
\toprule
$X_1$ & $X_2$ & $X_1 \times X_2$ & \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 1 & both reads are our guide\\
1 & 0 & 0 & \\
0 & 1 & 0 & \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
So the average of $X_1 \times X_2$ is $1 \times$ (chance both are our guide)
$+\; 0 \times$ (the other chances) $=$ the chance that both are our guide.
In case B: $0.05 - 0.04 - 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.05 - 0.04 = 0.01$.
Now put in Step~1. The chance that both reads are our guide is the average
of $p \times p$, which we write as the average of $p^2$. So
\[
\text{covariance} = \text{average of } p^2 - \mu^2 .
\]
Case B: $0.05 - 0.04 = 0.01$.

Last, we show that the right side is the wobble $V$. Start from the
definition of $V$ and open the square:
\begin{align*}
V &= \text{average of } (p - \mu) \times (p - \mu)
 && \text{(definition of } V\text{)}\\
&= \text{average of } \bigl(p^2 - 2 \times \mu \times p + \mu^2\bigr)
 && \text{(multiply out)}\\
&= \text{average of } p^2 - 2 \times \mu \times (\text{average of } p) + \mu^2
 && \text{(average each part)}\\
&= \text{average of } p^2 - 2 \times \mu^2 + \mu^2
 && \text{(the average of } p \text{ is } \mu\text{)}\\
&= \text{average of } p^2 - \mu^2 .
\end{align*}
Case B: $0.05 - 2 \times 0.2 \times 0.2 + 0.04 = 0.05 - 0.08 + 0.04 = 0.01$.

The covariance and $V$ are both equal to the average of $p^2 - \mu^2$. So
\[
\text{covariance of two reads} = V .
\]
In case B both are 0.01. The wobble of the proportion
\emph{is} the covariance of two reads.

\textbf{Step 3: the correlation.} The correlation is the covariance divided
by the standard deviations of the two values.\footnote{This is Pearson's
correlation coefficient: covariance $\div$ (standard deviation of value 1
$\times$ standard deviation of value 2).} Each read has the variance
$\mu \times (1-\mu)$ (Part~2), so each standard deviation is
$\sqrt{\mu \times (1-\mu)}$. The product of the two standard deviations is
$\mu \times (1-\mu)$. So
\[
\rho = \frac{\text{covariance}}{\text{SD of read 1} \times \text{SD of read 2}}
 = \frac{V}{\mu \times (1-\mu)} .
\]
Check with case B: $\rho = 0.01 \div 0.16 = 0.0625$. A computer drew
100{,}000 pairs of reads from case-B replicates and found the correlation
0.06. In case C, the two reads are always the same (both 0 or both 1), and
the correlation is 1.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\input{\guideroot common/figs/fig_rho.tex}
\caption{$\rho$, the correlation of two reads from the same replicate. It is
0 with no wobble and 1 with the largest possible wobble,
$\mu \times (1-\mu)$. In between, $\rho$ tells how full the ``meter'' is.}
\label{fig:rho}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Why $\rho$ is never more than 1.} A correlation cannot be more
than 1. Here this means that the wobble can never be more than
$\mu \times (1-\mu)$. The table shows why, with checks for case B and case
C.

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r@{\;}l l l@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Case B} ($p = 0.1, 0.3$) & \textbf{Case C} ($p = 0, 1$)\\
\midrule
a. & $V = $ average of $p^2 - \mu^2$ & $0.05 - 0.04 = 0.01$ & $0.2 - 0.04 = 0.16$\\
b. & $p \times p \le p$ for every $p$ from 0 to 1 & $0.01 \le 0.1$, $0.09 \le 0.3$ & $0 = 0$, $1 = 1$\\
c. & so: average of $p^2 \le \mu$ & $0.05 \le 0.2$ & $0.2 = 0.2$\\
d. & so: $V \le \mu - \mu^2 = \mu \times (1-\mu)$ & $0.01 \le 0.16$ & $0.16 = 0.16$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Line a.} Open the square:
  $(p-\mu)^2 = p^2 - 2 \times \mu \times p + \mu^2$. Take the average. The
  average of $p$ is $\mu$, so the middle part becomes
  $2 \times \mu \times \mu$. The result is: average of
  $p^2 - 2 \times \mu^2 + \mu^2 = $ average of $p^2 - \mu^2$.
\item \textbf{Line b.} A number from 0 to 1 does not grow when you multiply
  it by itself: $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$. It stays the same only for 0 and 1.
\item \textbf{Line c.} Each $p^2$ is at most $p$. So the average of $p^2$ is
  at most the average of $p$, which is $\mu$.
\item \textbf{Line d.} Put line c into line a.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Turn the formula around.} Multiply both sides of the formula for
$\rho$ by $\mu \times (1-\mu)$:
\[
\rho = \frac{V}{\mu \times (1-\mu)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{covariance of two reads} = V = \rho \times \mu \times (1-\mu).
\]
Check with case B: $0.0625 \times 0.16 = 0.01$. We use this in Part~4.

\begin{worked}[ 4d: the wobble of our guide]
\textbf{Variance of one read:} $\mu \times (1-\mu) = 0.000191 \times
0.999809 = 0.000191$.

\textbf{Correlation of two reads:} BARCS estimates $\rho = 0.000000386$
(Step~7 in Chapter~2 shows how). Two reads from the same library are almost
unrelated.

\textbf{The wobble (= covariance):} $V = \rho \times \mu \times (1-\mu) =
0.000000386 \times 0.000191 = 0.0000000000737$.

\textbf{In words:} the square root of $V$ is $0.0000086$, which is 8.6 per
million. So the true proportion of our guide moves by about 8.6 per million
between replicates, for example from 191 to 183 or 199 per million.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Part 4: Add up the reads --- where $N \times (N-1)$ comes from}

The count $K$ is the number of reads that are our guide. So $K$ is a sum of
$N$ values, one for each read: $K = X_1 + X_2 + \dots + X_N$, where each
$X$ is 1 (our guide) or 0 (not our guide).

\paragraph{Two reads first.} Part~2 (Step~B) said that the variances of
\emph{independent} values add up. But reads from the same replicate are not
independent: they move together. Look at the distance of the sum from its
average. It is the sum of the two distances, $d_1 + d_2$. Square it:
\[
(d_1 + d_2)^2 = d_1 \times d_1 + d_2 \times d_2 + 2 \times d_1 \times d_2 .
\]
Take the average of each part. The average of $d_1 \times d_1$ is the
variance of read 1. The average of $d_1 \times d_2$ is the covariance
(Part~3, Step~2). So
\[
\text{variance of } (X_1 + X_2) = \text{variance} + \text{variance} + 2 \times \text{covariance}.
\]
For independent reads the covariance is 0, and we get Step~B again.

\begin{worked}[ 4e: two reads from a case-B replicate]
Variance of one read: $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$. Covariance: $V = 0.01$.

Variance of the sum: $0.16 + 0.16 + 2 \times 0.01 = 0.34$.

A computer drew 100{,}000 pairs and found 0.340.
\end{worked}

\paragraph{$N$ reads.} Now square the sum of $N$ distances,
$(d_1 + d_2 + \dots + d_N)^2$. Multiply every part by every part. You get
two kinds of products:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Squares} $d_1 \times d_1$, $d_2 \times d_2$, \dots: there are
  $N$ of them. Each has the average $\mu \times (1-\mu)$.
\item \textbf{Pairs} $d_1 \times d_2$, $d_2 \times d_1$, \dots: each read
  pairs with each of the $N-1$ other reads. So there are
  $N \times (N-1)$ of them. Each has the average covariance $V$.
\end{itemize}
Example with $N = 3$ reads: the pairs are 1--2, 1--3, 2--1, 2--3, 3--1,
3--2. That is $6 = 3 \times 2$ pairs. (With two reads, there are
$2 \times 1 = 2$ pairs. That is the ``$2 \times$'' above.)

So
\[
\text{variance} = \underbrace{N \times \mu \times (1-\mu)}_{N \text{ squares}}
 + \underbrace{N \times (N-1) \times V}_{N \times (N-1) \text{ pairs}} .
\]
\textbf{This is where $N \times (N-1)$ comes from: it is the number of pairs
of reads.} Every pair of reads from the same replicate adds its covariance
$V$.

\paragraph{The final form.} Put in $V = \rho \times \mu \times (1-\mu)$ from
Part~3:
\[
\text{variance} = N \times \mu \times (1-\mu)
 + N \times (N-1) \times \rho \times \mu \times (1-\mu).
\]
Both parts contain $N \times \mu \times (1-\mu)$. Take it out, as in
$a + a \times b = a \times (1+b)$:
\[
\boxed{\;\text{variance} = \underbrace{N \times \mu \times (1-\mu)}_{\text{counting noise}}
 \times \underbrace{\bigl(1 + (N-1) \times \rho\bigr)}_{\text{extra factor from biological noise}}\;}
\]
Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the whole path.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
{\small

\newcommand{\cR}{\textcolor{barcsblue}{\rho}}%
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}%
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Step} & \textbf{Formula} \quad / \quad \textit{check with case B, $N = 10$}\\
\midrule

 & \textit{$\cV = 0.01$}\\
2. Covariance of two reads & covariance $=$ (both are our guide) $- \mu \times \mu = \cV$\\
 & \textit{$0.05 - 0.04 = 0.01$}\\
3. Correlation of two reads & $\cR = $ covariance $\div$ (SD $\times$ SD) $= \cV \div \bigl(\mu \times (1-\mu)\bigr)$\\

 & \textit{$0.0625 \times 0.16 = 0.01$}\\

\begin{worked}[ 4f: check with ten reads]
Case B ($\mu = 0.2$, $V = 0.01$, $\rho = 0.0625$) with $N = 10$ reads.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item Squares: $10 \times 0.16 = 1.6$.
\item Pairs: $10 \times 9 = 90$ pairs, each $0.01$: $90 \times 0.01 = 0.9$.
\item Total: $1.6 + 0.9 = 2.5$.
\item Boxed formula: $1.6 \times (1 + 9 \times 0.0625) = 1.6 \times 1.5625
  = 2.5$.
\end{itemize}
A computer that drew 100{,}000 such replicates found the variance 2.498.
With $N \times N$ in place of $N \times (N-1)$, the formula would give 2.6,
which is wrong: a read does not form a pair with itself.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}[ 4g: the total variance of our guide]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item Squares: $1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.000191 \times 0.999809 = 191.0$.
\item Pairs: $1{,}000{,}000 \times 999{,}999 \times 0.0000000000737 = 73.7$.
\item Total: $191.0 + 73.7 = 264.7$.
\item Boxed formula: $191 \times (1 + 999{,}999 \times 0.000000386) = 191
  \times 1.386 = 264.7$.
\end{itemize}
Each pair adds very little, but there are almost a million million pairs.

\textbf{Standard deviation:} $\sqrt{264.7} = 16.3$ reads. Counting noise
alone gives $\sqrt{191} = 13.8$ reads. Biological noise adds 39\% to the
variance.
\end{worked}

\begin{remember}
Look at the extra factor $1 + (N-1) \times \rho$. It contains $N$. When the
machine reads more, $N$ grows, and the factor grows too. So more reading
does not remove biological noise. Only more replicates help with biological
noise.
\end{remember}

\section{Step 5: The design matrix}
%=====================================================================

A \textbf{model} is a rule that predicts a value. BARCS predicts the
log-odds of a guide in each library. The model needs to know which group
each library belongs to. BARCS gets this information from the
\textbf{design matrix}.

\subsection{What a design matrix is}

A design matrix is a table of numbers. It has:
\begin{itemize}
\item one \textbf{row} for each library, and
\item one \textbf{column} for each $\beta$ value in the model.
\end{itemize}
We write the design matrix with the letter $X$.

For our four libraries, the model has two $\beta$ values. So $X$ has four
rows and two columns:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc|cc}
\toprule
Library & Group & Column 1: ``intercept'' & Column 2: ``drug''\\
\midrule
1 & control & 1 & 0\\
2 & control & 1 & 0\\
3 & drug    & 1 & 1\\
4 & drug    & 1 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{symbols}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l p{0.74\linewidth}@{}}
$X$ & The design matrix. One row for each library, one column for each
      $\beta$.\\
Intercept & A column of 1s. It belongs to $\beta_0$, the starting level.
      Every library gets the starting level.\\
Drug & A column that is 1 for a drug library and 0 for a control library.
      It belongs to $\beta_1$, the effect of the drug.\\
$\beta_0$ & ``beta zero''. The log-odds of the guide in the control group.\\
$\beta_1$ & ``beta one''. The change in log-odds from control to drug. This
      is the \textbf{effect}. It is the most important number.\\
\end{tabular}
\end{symbols}

\subsection{From the design matrix to a prediction}

To get the prediction for one library, take the row of that library. Multiply
each number in the row by its $\beta$. Then add the products:
\[
\text{predicted log-odds} = (\text{column 1}) \times \beta_0 +
(\text{column 2}) \times \beta_1 .
\]
This calculation is a \textbf{matrix multiplication}. You do it one row at
a time. We call the predicted log-odds $\eta$ (``eta'').

\begin{worked}[ 5: predictions from the design matrix]
BARCS finds $\beta_0 = -8.565$ and $\beta_1 = -0.542$ (Step~6 in Chapter~2 shows how).

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
Library 1 (row 1, 0): & $1 \times (-8.565) + 0 \times (-0.542)$ & $= -8.565$\\
Library 2 (row 1, 0): & $1 \times (-8.565) + 0 \times (-0.542)$ & $= -8.565$\\
Library 3 (row 1, 1): & $1 \times (-8.565) + 1 \times (-0.542)$ & $= -9.107$\\
Library 4 (row 1, 1): & $1 \times (-8.565) + 1 \times (-0.542)$ & $= -9.107$\\
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
The control libraries get $\beta_0$ only. The drug libraries get
$\beta_0 + \beta_1$. So $\beta_1$ is the difference between the groups.
\end{worked}

\subsection{From log-odds back to counts}

A prediction in log-odds is hard to read. So we change it back into a
proportion, and then into a count. The rule is:
\[
\mu = \frac{e^{\eta}}{1 + e^{\eta}}, \qquad
\text{expected count} = N \times \mu .
\]
This rule undoes the log-odds of Step~3.

\begin{worked}[ 6: expected counts]
\textbf{Control libraries} ($\eta = -8.565$):
$e^{-8.565} = 0.000190676$. Then
$\mu = 0.000190676 \div 1.000190676 = 0.000190640$, which is 190.6 per
million.

\textbf{Drug libraries} ($\eta = -9.107$):
$e^{-9.107} = 0.000110840$. Then $\mu = 0.000110827$, which is 110.8 per
million.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Library & $N$ & $\mu$ (per million) & Expected count $N \times \mu$ & Real count $K$\\
\midrule
1 & 1{,}000{,}000 & 190.6 & 190.6 & 180\\
2 & 1{,}200{,}000 & 190.6 & 228.8 & 240\\
3 & 1{,}000{,}000 & 110.8 & 110.8 & 120\\
4 &   800{,}000 & 110.8 &  88.7 &  80\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
The expected counts are near the real counts. The differences are
$-10.6$, $+11.2$, $+9.2$ and $-8.7$ reads. These differences are the noise.
\end{worked}

\subsection{The reference group}

The control group has 0 in the drug column. So control is the
\textbf{reference group}. The effect $\beta_1$ always compares a group with
the reference group. If you make drug the reference group, $\beta_1$ changes
sign. It becomes $+0.542$. The meaning stays the same.

\subsection{Paired replicates: add a column}

Sometimes the replicates are pairs. For example, replicate~1 of control and
replicate~1 of drug come from the same batch of cells. Then BARCS can remove
the batch difference. You add a column for the batch:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc|ccc}
\toprule
Library & Group & Replicate & Intercept & Replicate 2 & Drug\\
\midrule
1 & control & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
2 & control & 2 & 1 & 1 & 0\\
3 & drug    & 1 & 1 & 0 & 1\\
4 & drug    & 2 & 1 & 1 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Now the model has three $\beta$ values: the starting level, the batch
difference and the drug effect. Each new column costs one degree of freedom
(Step~8 in Chapter~2). With four libraries and three columns, only one degree of freedom
stays.

\begin{worked}[ 7: the paired model for our guide]
BARCS gives the drug effect $\beta = -0.544$ with standard error 0.143.
The effect is almost the same as before ($-0.542$). But the degrees of
freedom drop from 2 to 1. The p-value becomes 0.164 instead of 0.033.

Our two replicates have almost no batch difference ($\beta = 0.005$). So
the extra column costs information and gives nothing back. Add a batch
column only when the replicates really are pairs.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Numbers instead of groups}

A column can also hold a number, for example the day of the experiment.
Suppose that you collect cells on day 0, day 7 and day 14, two libraries
each. You put the day divided by 14 into a column:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc|cc}
\toprule
Library & Day & Intercept & Day $\div$ 14\\
\midrule
1, 2 & 0  & 1 & 0\\
3, 4 & 7  & 1 & 0.5\\
5, 6 & 14 & 1 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Now $\beta_1$ is the change in log-odds over 14 days. The model draws a
straight line through the three days.

\begin{worked}[ 8: a time course]
Suppose that $\beta_1 = -0.70$.

After 14 days: change $= 1 \times (-0.70) = -0.70$. Fold change
$= e^{-0.70} = 0.50$. The guide drops to half.

After 7 days: change $= 0.5 \times (-0.70) = -0.35$. Fold change
$= e^{-0.35} = 0.70$. The guide drops to 70\%.

The model has 6 libraries and 2 columns. So it has $6 - 2 = 4$ degrees of
freedom.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Two factors and an interaction}

Some experiments change two things. For example, cells can be normal or can
have a second gene switched off (``knockout''). And cells can get no drug or
the drug. This experiment has four groups. The design matrix has four
columns:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|cccc}
\toprule
Group & Intercept & Knockout & Drug & Knockout $\times$ Drug\\
\midrule
normal, no drug   & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
knockout, no drug & 1 & 1 & 0 & 0\\
normal, drug      & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0\\
knockout, drug    & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last column is the product of the knockout column and the drug column.
It is 1 only when both are 1. Its $\beta$ is the \textbf{interaction}. The
interaction measures the extra change that happens only when both changes
happen together.

\begin{worked}[ 9: reading an interaction]
Suppose that the four $\beta$ values are $-8.5$, $-0.2$, $+0.1$ and
$-0.7$. Multiply each row by the $\beta$ values and add:

\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
normal, no drug: & $-8.5$\\
knockout, no drug: & $-8.5 + (-0.2) = -8.7$\\
normal, drug: & $-8.5 + 0.1 = -8.4$\\
knockout, drug: & $-8.5 + (-0.2) + 0.1 + (-0.7) = -9.3$\\
\end{tabular}

\smallskip
The knockout alone changes the log-odds by $-0.2$. The drug alone changes it
by $+0.1$. If the two effects only added up, the last group would be
$-8.5 - 0.2 + 0.1 = -8.6$. The real value is $-9.3$. The extra $-0.7$ is the
interaction. The guide drops strongly only when both changes happen.
\end{worked}

\subsection{Comparing two treatments: a contrast}

Suppose an experiment has three groups: control, drug A and drug B. The
design matrix has the columns intercept, drug A and drug B. Then $\beta_A$
compares drug A with control, and $\beta_B$ compares drug B with control. To
compare drug A with drug B, you subtract: $\beta_A - \beta_B$. This
calculation is a \textbf{contrast}. In BARCS, the function
\texttt{bb\_contrast()} calculates it and its test.

For example, if $\beta_A = -0.9$ and $\beta_B = -0.4$, then
$\beta_A - \beta_B = -0.5$. Drug A lowers the guide more than drug B.

\subsection{Rules for a good design matrix}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Make one row for each library, in the same order as the count table.
\item Make one column for each $\beta$ that you want.
\item Keep more rows than columns. The difference is the degrees of
  freedom. Two or more is better than one.
\item Do not let two columns carry the same information. For example,
  suppose that all drug libraries come from batch~2. Suppose also that all
  control libraries come from batch~1. Then the batch column and the drug
  column are equal. BARCS cannot separate the drug effect from the batch
  effect.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The design matrix in R}

In R, you describe the design matrix with a \textbf{formula}. The formula
\texttt{\textasciitilde\ group} means ``an intercept and a column for each
group except the reference group''.

The function \texttt{model.matrix()} builds $X$ from the formula:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.86\textwidth}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
> data <- data.frame(group = c("control", "control", "drug", "drug"))
> model.matrix(~ group, data)
  (Intercept) groupdrug
1           1         0
2           1         0
3           1         1
4           1         1
\end{verbatim}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

Then you give the counts, the totals, the formula and the column name to
BARCS:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.86\textwidth}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
fit <- bb_screen(counts, data, formula = ~ group,
                 term = "groupdrug", totals = totals)
\end{verbatim}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

For the paired design, use the formula \texttt{\textasciitilde\ replicate +
group}. For the interaction, use \texttt{\textasciitilde\ knockout * drug}.

\section{Chapter 1 summary}
%=====================================================================

Chapter~1 showed what BARCS measures and how it describes an experiment:
\begin{enumerate}
\item It turns read counts into proportions (Step~1).
\item It compares groups with a fold change and its logarithm (Step~2).
\item It works on the log-odds scale (Step~3).
\item It separates counting noise from biological noise (Step~4).
\item It describes the experiment with a design matrix (Step~5).
\end{enumerate}
Chapter~2 shows how BARCS calculates the effect and decides if it is real.
