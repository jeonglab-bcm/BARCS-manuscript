Many pooled CRISPR screens no longer ask only whether guide abundance differs
between a control group and one treatment group.  Libraries may be collected
across time, dose, ordered FACS bins, donors, batches, or combinations of these
variables.  The scientific target is then a trend, adjusted association,
interaction, or prespecified contrast.  Dichotomizing the design into
``high'' and ``low'' groups discards intermediate information and makes
covariate adjustment opaque.

Pooled-screen tools inherited their count distribution from RNA-seq, where read
counts were first treated as Poisson \citep{marioni2008rnaseq} and then, to
admit overdispersion, as negative binomial
\citep{anders2010deseq,love2014deseq2}; several widely used sgRNA tools adopted
the negative binomial directly \citep{li2014mageck,spahn2017pinaplpy}.  Screen
counts are not structured like transcript counts, however: transcript lengths
span orders of magnitude, whereas sgRNAs are synthesized to a single fixed
length.  CB\textsuperscript{2} therefore modeled each guide count as
beta-binomial against its library total, and demonstrated that the
beta-binomial fits CRISPR pooled-screen data better than the negative binomial
\citep{jeong2019beta}.

The limitation was never that variance model but the mean model beside it.  The
statistical construction for regression on overdispersed sequencing counts has
existed since \citet{baggerly2004overdispersed}, but no CRISPR-screen
implementation has made the beta-binomial available beyond a comparison of two
groups.
A time course, a dose series, a set of ordered FACS bins, or a donor-adjusted
contrast must first be collapsed into ``high'' and ``low'' before the
better-fitting likelihood can be used at all, so an analyst with a quantitative
design has had to choose between the right variance model and the right mean
model.  Closing that gap requires only the same beta-binomial hierarchy under
an arbitrary design matrix.

Stated concretely, for guide $g$ suppose we observe counts $K_{gi}$, full
mapped-guide totals $N_i$, and one row $\mathbf{x}_i^\top$ of a full-rank
design matrix for each independent biological screen unit $i$, represented by a
sequenced library.  We want to estimate
\begin{equation}
  \logit(\mu_{gi})
  =
  \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_g
  \quad\text{and test}\quad
  H_0:\mathbf{c}^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}_g=0,
  \label{eq:intro-target}
\end{equation}
while allowing unequal library depths and extra-binomial variation.  The
output should be an interpretable guide coefficient, standard error, and
$p$-value for the scientific contrast selected before examining the guide
results.

Terminology matters: guide count or proportion is the response in
Equation~\eqref{eq:intro-target}.  Time, dose, treatment, or an ordered
phenotype summary enters as a sample-level predictor.  BARCS does not analyze
a continuous per-cell outcome unless that outcome has first been observed or
validly summarized at the library level.

Four familiar approaches fall short of the target in
Equation~\eqref{eq:intro-target}, each for a different reason, in rough order
of how close they come.  Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:problem} states the same
specification component by component, including what the model does not
identify.

\emph{Regressing on individual reads misidentifies the information unit.}
Each read becomes an observation, so residual degrees of freedom grow with
sequencing depth and a library sequenced twice as deeply appears to carry twice
the biological evidence.  It carries no additional biological evidence; it
remains a single screen unit, measured more precisely.

\emph{Repeating a two-group test misspecifies the estimand.}  Separate pairwise
contrasts cannot express a single coefficient adjusted for the remaining
columns of $X$, and splitting an ordered design into pairs discards the
intermediate levels that motivated collecting it.

\emph{General RNA-seq count models satisfy both requirements and differ in the
treatment of depth.}  edgeR and limma--voom fit a design matrix and test contrasts,
but they enter library size as an offset or as a precision weight on a
normalized scale \citep{lun2016edger,law2014voom}.  A guide count is instead a
draw from a known total, and using $N_i$ as that total is what splits the
variance into a sequencing term that shrinks with depth and a between-library
term that does not, as in Equation~\eqref{eq:propvar}.

\emph{Specialist screen models lie at the opposite extreme.}  Chronos and
Waterbear answer richer questions---growth dynamics, joint bin
membership---but each is built around one experimental structure, and neither
returns an arbitrary prespecified coefficient or contrast as its output.

What has changed in the seven years since CB\textsuperscript{2}
\citep{jeong2019beta} is not the estimating equation but the evidence available
to test a multiple-group extension.  Each
dataset that discriminates one is recent: deposited longitudinal fitness screens
with a joint population-dynamics model to compare against
\citep{dempster2021chronos}, an ordered-bin FACS screen carrying 26
individually knockout-validated regulators alongside hundreds of non-targeting
guides \citep{freimer2022il2ra,pimentel2024waterbear}, a simulator that gives
every guide a genuine interaction rather than one scalar effect
\citep{zhu2026simcrispr}, and transcriptome-scale Cas13 screens with
cell-line-matched null controls \citep{liang2026lncrna}.  Seven years ago a
quantitative-design extension could be argued from first principles; it can now
be settled per design class against validated biology.  The designs in which
BARCS is outperformed are reported here as part of that assessment.

BARCS---Beta-binomial Analysis and Regression for CRISPR Screens---keeps the
better-fitting likelihood and replaces the two group means with a design
matrix.  It models each guide count against the corresponding full-library
total, uses a beta-binomial variance to separate sequencing precision from
between-library heterogeneity, and places continuous variables, factors,
interactions, and covariates in an ordinary R model matrix.  Independent
libraries, not read depth or guide number, determine the sample-level
reference degrees of freedom.  The evaluation below therefore concentrates on
designs a two-group test cannot express---ordered bins, a factorial
interaction, and a genome-scale sorted screen---and treats the longitudinal
case as a check that nothing is lost where specialist methods already
apply.

The manuscript makes four contributions.  First, it defines the quantitative
pooled-screen target as a coefficient or contrast rather than a forced binary
comparison.  Second, it establishes precisely how that regression framework
relates to the original CB\textsuperscript{2} statistic: exact nesting on the
legacy group-summary scale and first-order local equivalence for the default
logit fit under stated asymptotics, not finite-sample identity.  Third, it
provides control-based diagnostics and an optional shared-effect gene
statistic without making Fisher or Stouffer aggregation part of the core
method.  Fourth, it evaluates both the intended use case and failure
boundaries against MAGeCK \citep{li2014mageck,li2015mageckvispr}, Chronos
\citep{dempster2021chronos}, BAGEL2 \citep{kim2021bagel2}, Waterbear
\citep{pimentel2024waterbear}, MAUDE \citep{deboer2020maude}, CRISPhieRmix
\citep{daley2018crisphiermix}, and the published
\texttt{RobustRankAggreg} analysis of \citet{liang2026lncrna}
\citep{kolde2012rra}, which is a rank aggregation distinct from MAGeCK-RRA.

The scope is deliberately narrower than continuous per-cell phenotype
analysis.  BARCS analyzes a quantitative coordinate attached to each
biological screen unit.  It cannot reconstruct a continuous phenotype for
each cell when guide identity and phenotype were never observed jointly.  It
also does not make correlated FACS bins independent, infer mechanistic
viability dynamics, correct copy number automatically, or replace missing
biological replication.  Those boundaries determine the benchmark design and
the method choice rules reported below.
