\subsection{Genome-scale simulation separates ranking from calibrated
discovery}
\label{sec:crispulator}

We used CRISPulator to evaluate a genome-scale FACS design under known truth
\citep{nagy2017crispulator}.  Three prespecified seeds each generated 10,000
genes, five guides per gene, four independent replicates, and low, bulk, and
high fractions.  Twenty percent of genes affected the phenotype and 5\% were
explicit negative controls.  All methods received the same simulated counts
and design.

Two BARCS statistics were evaluated.  BARCS-ST uses the original guide-wise
dispersion estimates and a calibrated signed-$z$ gene summary.  BARCS-MOD
moderates each guide's dispersion toward an abundance-dependent trend before
the same gene summary.  The comparison methods were MAGeCK-MLE and
CRISPhieRmix, two CRISPR-specific gene callers with different operating
characteristics \citep{li2015mageckvispr,daley2018crisphiermix}.

The methods ranked genes similarly but converted those rankings into
discoveries differently.  MAGeCK-MLE had the
highest average precision and effect correlation, although its average
precision exceeded BARCS-MOD by only 0.002.  It used little of the permitted
error budget (FDP 0.006) and consequently recovered fewer active genes.
CRISPhieRmix attained nearly the same recall as BARCS-MOD but at FDP 0.197.
BARCS-MOD occupied the intermediate and intended operating point: recall 0.774
at FDP 0.066, yielding the highest F1.

Dispersion moderation improved both ranking and decision performance without
changing the point estimates.  Average precision increased from 0.902 to
0.919 and F1 from 0.811 to 0.847, while realized FDP remained essentially
unchanged.  The moderation prior was estimated from 50,000 guides, providing
42.7--45.8 prior degrees of freedom in addition to seven residual degrees of
freedom per guide.  This library-wide information stabilizes noisy
guide-specific variance estimates without treating reads as biological
replicates.

Because a fixed nominal threshold can place methods at different realized
error rates, we also interpolated F1 at matched FDP.  BARCS-MOD led at every
shared operating point: its F1 was 0.640, 0.724, 0.764, 0.808, and 0.838 at
realized FDP 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, and 0.05, respectively.  The
corresponding ranges across the other methods were 0.372--0.491,
0.488--0.666, 0.553--0.739, 0.640--0.789, and 0.725--0.795.  MAGeCK-MLE did
not reach FDP 0.05 on the scanned grid because it remained more conservative.

The result was reproduced at MOI 0.30: BARCS-MOD reached F1 0.841 at FDP
0.070, CRISPhieRmix reached comparable recall at FDP 0.217, and MAGeCK-MLE
again ranked marginally best while calling least.  These simulations therefore
support the calibrated-discovery performance of BARCS.  They also show why
the beta-binomial model alone is insufficient:
the practical gain depends on stable dispersion estimation and a threshold
whose realized error rate is measured.
